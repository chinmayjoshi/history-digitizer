The Two Battles of Tarain — Defeat, Dishonour, and One Sunset Charge

Source: printed pp. 151–155 (scans 245–249) · 9 comic panels

1191. When Mahummud Ghori marched into Hindostan, the Rajput confederacy under the Chauhan king — the book's 'Pittu Rai' (Prithviraj) — met him near Thanesar with 200,000 horse and 3,000 elephants. The Sultan's wings were outflanked and driven in until his army fought as a circle. Advised to flee, he struck the adviser and charged instead — and was spotted by the Raja of Delhi, who drove his elephant straight at him. Mahummud hurled his lance with such force it knocked out three of the elephant's back teeth; the Raja's spear went through his right arm. As he sank from his horse, a faithful servant leaped up behind him and carried him from the field. The rout was pursued for forty miles. Back in Ghor, the Sultan publicly disgraced every Omrah who had deserted him. 1192. A sage petitioned him: let the disgraced Omrahs wipe away their stains. He sent for them, robed them in honour, and returned to the very same field — where Pittu Rai now waited with 300,000 Rajput horse, 3,000 elephants and 150 Rajas who had sworn their war-oaths with the tica on their foreheads. All day Mahummud attacked by divisions, wheeling off in feigned retreats, 'deluding them with a security of victory.' Then, as the sun touched the west, he put himself at the head of 12,000 steel-clad horse and made one resolute charge: 'this prodigious army, once shaken, like a great building, was lost in its own ruins.' The Raja of Delhi fell in the field; Pittu Rai was taken in the pursuit and put to death; Ajmer was stormed. The Sultan left his slave-general Cuttub ul dien Abeik behind with an army — the seed from which the whole Delhi Sultanate would grow.

The Empress Rizia — “No Fault But That She Was a Woman”

Source: printed pp. 183–186 (scans 277–280) · 9 comic panels

Delhi, 1236. When Sultan Iltutmish looked at his sons — given up, in his words, to 'wine, women, gaming, and the worship of the wind' — he trained his eldest daughter Rizia in government instead, and left her as regent when he campaigned. After his death and a brother's six-month collapse, the nobles raised Rizia to the throne. She assumed the imperial robes, gave public audience daily, restored her father's laws and, the chronicler says, distributed justice 'with an equal hand'. When a coalition of provincial governors marched on Delhi, she beat them without a battle — sowing dissension among them until the alliance dissolved, then hunting down the ringleaders. Her undoing was favoritism: she raised Eacoot, an Abyssinian former slave, to Captain General over the Turkish nobles' fury. The governor of Tiberhind rebelled; on the march her own Turkish Omrahs mutinied, killed her general and seized her. In captivity her jailer Altunia married her, and together they raised an army of Gickers and Jits and twice marched on Delhi to win back her empire. Defeated both times by Balin, she and her husband were overtaken in flight and 'inhumanly put to immediate death'. Ferishta's verdict: 'Thus died the Empress Rizia, deserving a better fate.'

The Fall of Somnath — “A Breaker of Idols, Not a Seller”

Source: printed pp. 80–86 (scans 174–181) · 10 comic panels

In the year 415 of the Hegira, Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni mustered the full strength of his empire — 55,000 chosen horse, 1,300 elephants and 100,000 foot — and turned it toward Gujarat. His target was Sumnat (Somnath), a temple-fortress on a narrow sea-washed peninsula, fabulously rich, where devotees believed the souls of the dead came to be assigned their next bodies. The defenders crowded the battlements and sent a herald: their god himself had drawn the Muslims here to blast them in a moment. Mahmud 'only smiled at this vain threat' and ordered the assault at dawn. When his archers cleared the battlements, the defenders crowded into the temple to weep before the idol — and the attackers scaled the walls mid-prayer, crying 'Alla Akber!'. After a last rally in which Mahmud personally dragged his faltering general back into hope, the garrison broke: 5,000 lay dead, 4,000 fled by sea toward Ceylon. Inside the temple — a hall of fifty-six jewel-set pillars — the Sultan raised his mace and struck the nose off the five-yard stone idol. When the Brahmins offered crores in gold to spare it, and his own nobles urged him to take the ransom as charity, he refused: he would not be called a seller of idols; breaker was the more honourable title. The next blow split the idol's hollow belly — and out poured diamonds, rubies and pearls worth more than the ransom. The temple held gold and jewels 'than, it is thought, any royal treasury ever contained': a golden chain of forty maunds for the bell, 2,000 Brahmin priests, 500 dancing girls, 300 musicians, 300 barbers to shave the devotees, and Ganges water hauled five hundred miles for the god's daily bath. Fragments of the idol were carted to Ghazni to be laid at the threshold of the public mosque, with two more reserved for Mecca and Medina.
