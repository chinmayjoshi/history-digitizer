The Fall of Somnath — “A Breaker of Idols, Not a Seller”

Source pages: printed pp. 80–86 (scans 174–181) · Figures: Sultan Mamood (Mahmud of Ghazni), Hassen Chirkani, Raja Byram Deo, The Brahmins of Somnath

In the year 415 of the Hegira, Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni mustered the full strength of his empire — 55,000 chosen horse, 1,300 elephants and 100,000 foot — and turned it toward Gujarat. His target was Sumnat (Somnath), a temple-fortress on a narrow sea-washed peninsula, fabulously rich, where devotees believed the souls of the dead came to be assigned their next bodies. The defenders crowded the battlements and sent a herald: their god himself had drawn the Muslims here to blast them in a moment. Mahmud 'only smiled at this vain threat' and ordered the assault at dawn. When his archers cleared the battlements, the defenders crowded into the temple to weep before the idol — and the attackers scaled the walls mid-prayer, crying 'Alla Akber!'. After a last rally in which Mahmud personally dragged his faltering general back into hope, the garrison broke: 5,000 lay dead, 4,000 fled by sea toward Ceylon. Inside the temple — a hall of fifty-six jewel-set pillars — the Sultan raised his mace and struck the nose off the five-yard stone idol. When the Brahmins offered crores in gold to spare it, and his own nobles urged him to take the ransom as charity, he refused: he would not be called a seller of idols; breaker was the more honourable title. The next blow split the idol's hollow belly — and out poured diamonds, rubies and pearls worth more than the ransom. The temple held gold and jewels 'than, it is thought, any royal treasury ever contained': a golden chain of forty maunds for the bell, 2,000 Brahmin priests, 500 dancing girls, 300 musicians, 300 barbers to shave the devotees, and Ganges water hauled five hundred miles for the god's daily bath. Fragments of the idol were carted to Ghazni to be laid at the threshold of the public mosque, with two more reserved for Mecca and Medina.

Why it stands out: The raid on Somnath (c. 1025) is the single most famous episode of Mahmud's seventeen Indian expeditions — retold for a thousand years from every side. The book preserves the full set-piece: the herald's curse, the mid-prayer escalade, the ransom refused with a epigram, and the treasure hidden inside the idol itself. It is also a lesson in reading old histories critically: Ferishta's account is written unabashedly from the conqueror's side.

The story as a comic

1
The muster plain outside Ghazni: endless cavalry, war elephants, banners.
Mahmud: Fifty-five thousand horse. Thirteen hundred elephants. We march on Sumnat.
415 AH: the Sultan reviews the full might of his empire before his richest expedition yet.
2
A caravan of war snaking through the Gujarat desert toward the sea.
Through waterless country where an army can only march with water-carriers and prayer, they come at last in sight of the sea.
3
The temple-fortress on its peninsula, battlements black with defenders; a herald rides out.
Herald: Our god Sumnat has drawn you here to blast you in a moment!
Mahmud (smiling): At dawn, we assault.
The defenders are certain their god will destroy the invaders himself. 'The Sultan only smiled at this vain threat.'
4
Dawn: volleys of arrows sweeping the walls; ladders rising against the stone.
Scaling parties: Alla Akber!
As the defenders crowd into the temple to weep before the idol, the ladders go up — mid-prayer.
5
Chaos at the wall: Mahmud grips his wavering general Hassen Chirkani by the hand.
Mahmud: Shall we abandon the path of renown now? Forward!
The line nearly breaks; the Sultan rallies it himself. Five thousand defenders fall.
6
Boats crammed with fugitives pulling away from the rocks, pursued by the Sultan's rowers.
Four thousand of the garrison escape by sea toward Ceylon — 'but they did not escape the eyes of the Sultan.'
7
The temple hall: fifty-six jeweled pillars; before the great stone idol, Mahmud raises his mace.
Mahmud: (strikes)
With one blow he strikes the nose from the five-yard idol.
8
A deputation of Brahmins kneeling with coffers of gold; the Sultan turns away.
Brahmins: Crores of gold, O King, if you proceed no further!
Mahmud: A seller of idols? No — a breaker of them is the more honourable title.
The ransom refused — against even his own nobles' advice.
9
The idol's belly splits open, spilling gems across the floor.
The next blow reveals the idol is hollow — full of diamonds, rubies and pearls worth more than the ransom offered.
10
Pack animals laden with gold; two fragments of stone bound for distant cities.
The spoils go to Ghazni; fragments of the idol are laid at the threshold of its mosque, with two more sent to Mecca and Medina.
