The places of the book

Cities, frontiers, rivers and holy places on Firishta's stage

The places where the story unfolds, with their modern settings.

Ghizni

Ghazni, Afghanistan

Capital of the Ghaznavid Empire

In the book: The 'Empire of Ghizni' of Part II: seat of Sabuktigin's slaves, of Mahmud's minting, and of the golden throne whose mosque threshold received fragments of the Somnath idol.

Editor's note: Today a small Afghan city, Ghazni was briefly one of the great cultural and military capitals of Asia under Mahmud — a centre of Persian poetry and the springboard for seventeen raids into India.

Hindostan

India (the Indo-Gangetic plain chiefly)

The book's name for India

In the book: The whole stage: 'the History of the Hindoos before the first invasion… by the Mahomedans.' The name colours every page, from the fabulous kings to the eve of Timur.

Editor's note: In Dow's and Firishta's usage 'Hindostan' specifically meant northern India — the basins of the Indus and Ganges — not the peninsula (the 'Decan'), which the Delhi sultans only intermittently reached.

Delhi

Delhi, India

Capital of the Sultanate; a city of seven successive capitals

In the book: Three Delhis meet here: the 'old city' of the Chauhans, the palace of Seri (Siri, Alauddin's city) and Firose-abad — the stage for the Slave, Khalji and Tughlaq sultans, and finally the two kings warring within one capital.

Editor's note: By the late 14th century Sultanate Delhi sprawled across several walled cities. Timur, entering in 1398, is reported to have been astonished by the gardens — and to have depopulated the city in three days of massacre.

Moultan

Multan, Punjab, Pakistan

Frontier gateway and siege city

In the book: Time and again the hinge of war: besieged six months by Timur's grandson, contested between the Tughlaq governors, and the refuge of princes and pretenders throughout Part III.

Editor's note: Multan, on the ancient trade route to Central Asia, has been a fortified prize for millennia — one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities of the subcontinent and, then, a stronghold of Sunni learning.

Lahore

Lahore, Pakistan

Ghaznavid frontier capital; refuge of defeated sultans

In the book: Where Muhammad Ghori recovered from his wound after the first Tarain defeat, and where pretenders and exiles repeatedly gathered armies to march on Delhi.

Editor's note: Lahore was Mahmud's winter capital and, for the Ghaznavids, the western seat from which India was governed — a role the city would replay for empires for nine hundred years.

Sumnat

Somnath, Gujarat, India

The great temple destroyed by Mahmud (c. 1025/26)

In the book: The sea-girt 'lofty castle on a narrow peninsula' and the richest idol of Hindostan — whose destruction, Dow reports from Firishta, yielded treasure 'greater… than any royal treasury ever contained.'

Editor's note: Somnath has been destroyed and rebuilt many times over a millennium; a temple stands there today. Modern historians debate the raid's scale but not its reality — it remains one of the most contested events in Indian historiography.

The field of Tarain

Taraori, near Thanesar, Haryana, India

Scene of the two decisive battles (1191 & 1192)

In the book: Dow places it near 'Tannassar' on the 'Sirfutti' (Saraswati) river — first a Rajput victory, then, on the same field, the ruin of Pittu Rai's army of 300,000.

Editor's note: Tarain (Taraori) is one of the pivot-stones of Indian history: the second battle ended Hindu supremacy over the north and opened it to the Delhi Sultanate.

Ajmere

Ajmer, Rajasthan, India

Capital of Prithviraj and the Chauhans

In the book: Where Ghori sent his embassy 'with a declaration of war if they should reject the true faith', and whose fall — with 'thousands put to the sword' and the rest enslaved — followed the second battle of Tarain.

Editor's note: Ajmer was a Rajput stronghold on the trade route to the west; its conquest, like Tarain, brought Rajputana within reach of Delhi — an omen of the long struggle between the sultans and the great Rajput house of Mewar.

Tiberhind

Tabarhinda (Bhatinda), Punjab, India

The frontier fort of Razia's imprisonment and marriage

In the book: The fort to which the great Empress Rizia was sent after her army's mutiny — and where her captor and jailer Altunia married her, the strange alliance that twice marched on Delhi to win back her empire.

Editor's note: A minor fort on the desert frontier of Punjab, Tabarhinda enters history almost solely through Razia's last, doomed campaigns — a footnote fort made famous by a queen's fall.

The river Sind

The Indus

The great western barrier of Hindostan

In the book: The crossing-point of every invader from the north-west — Mahmud's armies, Ghori's, and finally Timur, whose crossing of the Sind is the last line of Vol. I.

Editor's note: Few rivers have been crossed by so many world-conquerors. The Indus was the pivot of Dow's whole scheme: everything east of it was 'Hindostan', and to cross it was to enter the history of India.

The Cheiber Pass

The Khyber Pass, Afghanistan/Pakistan border

The invasion gateway

In the book: Mentioned as the 'fort of Cheiber in the passes of the mountains' built by the Afghan-Pathan tribes to guard the approaches of Hindostan — a barrier every north-western invader ultimately had to force.

Editor's note: The Khyber has funneled armies for over two thousand years — Alexander, Mahmud, Ghori, Babur, and later the British — making it the single most strategic defile in the region's history.

Sirindiep

Ceylon (Sri Lanka)

The island refuge of the Somnath garrison

In the book: Where the four thousand of Somnath's garrison fled by boat 'intending to proceed to the island of Sirindiep' after their walls fell — Dow's footnote glosses it simply: 'Ceylon.'

Editor's note: That defeated defenders of a temple in Gujarat would sail for Sri Lanka tells us how far Arab and Indian maritime trade had knit the Indian Ocean together by the 11th century.

The Ganges

The Ganga, India

The sacred river of the Hindoos

In the book: Mentioned with awe at Somnath — the idolaters washed the god 'every morning and evening, with fresh water from the Ganges, though that river is above five hundred crores distant.'

Editor's note: The water for the daily bath came from thousands of miles away — a measure both of the Ganges' sanctity and of the immense pilgrim routes that already crossed India in the 11th century.
