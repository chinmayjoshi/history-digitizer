The Slave Who Became King — the Rise of Sabuktigin

Source pages: printed pp. 39–41 (scans 132–135) · Figures: Subuctagi (Sabuktigin), Abistagi (Alptigin), Abu Isaac, Munsur of Bochara, Tigha of Bust

In the 10th century, the Samanid empire of Bokhara was run by Turkish slave-soldiers. When the boy-king Munsur took the throne, his greatest general, Abistagi, knew a summons home meant death — so he 'stood behind his disobedience with thirty thousand men,' marched to Ghazni, and raised his own standard of royalty. Among his officers was Subuctagi, originally a Turkish slave, who 'from a low degree raised himself by his valour' until he governed Chorassan and led the raids upon the Rajas of Hind. When Abistagi died, his son Abu Isaac — 'very young and addicted to pleasure' — gladly left the whole administration to Subuctigin, and 'soon travelled the road of mortality.' In the year 367 of the Hegira (977 AD), the Omrahs of Ghazni, 'who admired the wisdom and bravery of Subuctagi, unanimously elected him their King.' The slave had become a Sultan; he married his old master's daughter and 'turning his mind wholly upon the art of government, soon established justice in his dominions, and held the hearts of his subjects in his hand.' The book preserves one perfect parable of his rule: he restored the dispossessed chief Tigha to his fort on a promise of tribute; Tigha broke his word; the King dissembled, invited him to the chase, and alone with him, upbraided his ingratitude. Tigha drew his sword and wounded the King's hand — but the attendants poured in, the pursuit was so hot that the royal troops entered the fort at Tigha's own heels, and the ungrateful chief escaped only by leaping the wall and fleeing to Kirman. The dynasty this slave founded would, under his son Mahmud, become the first great Muslim empire of India.

Why it stands out: The Ghaznavid empire — the power that carried Islam deep into the subcontinent — was founded by an elected slave. His story is the book's cleanest illustration of the slave-soldier system that made medieval Islamic dynasties: merit could carry a man from the auction block to a crown, and the Tigha episode shows the personal, hunting-field style of early kingship.

The story as a comic

1
The court of Bokhara: the boy-king Munsur on the throne, courtiers whispering; General Abistagi at the door with his army.
Abistagi: A summons home is my death warrant. We march for Ghazni — thirty thousand strong.
Snubbed by the new king, the greatest general of Bokhara takes his army and founds his own kingdom.
2
Ghazni's citadel: a new royal standard rising; beside the general, a young officer — the slave Subuktigin.
Chronicler (caption): From a low degree, he raised himself by his valour.
In Abistagi's service, a Turkish slave rises to govern Chorassan and lead the raids upon the Rajas of Hind.
3
A palace of pleasures: young King Abu Isaac lounging while Subuktigin, at a desk of petitions, governs.
Abu Isaac: Affairs of state bore me. They are yours, Subuktagi.
The heir is 'very young and addicted to pleasure' — the slave runs the kingdom. Two years later, the king is dead.
4
367 AH: the Omrahs of Ghazni gathered, hands raised in acclamation toward Subuktigin.
The Omrahs: Let Subuktagi be King!
Admiring 'his wisdom and bravery,' the nobles unanimously elect the slave their king.
5
The new Sultan on the musnud, receiving his old master's daughter as bride; subjects below.
Chronicler (caption): He established justice, and held the hearts of his subjects in his hand.
The slave becomes Sultan — and by all accounts, a good one.
6
The hunting field: the King and the chief Tigha alone among the beaters; the King's hand bleeds; Tigha's sword half-drawn.
Subuktagi: I restored your fort. Where is your tribute — your gratitude?
Tigha: (draws his sword)
He restored an ungrateful chief, then called him to account alone in the chase — and took a sword-cut on the hand for it.
7
Tigha's fort: the fleeing chief scrambling over the wall as the King's cavalry thunders in at his heels.
The pursuit is so hot the royal troops enter the fort at his heels; Tigha escapes by leaping the wall — and vanishes toward Kirman.
