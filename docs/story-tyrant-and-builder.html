The Tyrant and the Builder — Death of a Despot, Rise of Firoz Shah

A sultan whom all men feared and abhorred dies of a fever after overeating fish — and the empire passes to a reforming builder.

The book gives Sultan Mahummud of the Tuglick house a tombstone of pure horror: 'this tyrant was conquered by death, and shut up in the dark dungeon of the grave. He reigned twenty-seven years, during which time he seems to have laboured, with no contemptible abilities, to be detested by God, and feared and abhorred by all men.' The man who moved his capital halfway across India, invented a system of state husbandry, and advanced singers, gardeners, weavers and slaves to command provinces — while his agents massacred eighty nobles at a banquet and were rewarded with robes — died prosaically of a fever brought on by eating fish to excess on the banks of the Indus. The omrahs, in the chaos after his death and the looting of his treasure by Mogul mercenaries, pressed his nephew Firoze Shaw onto the throne. Ferishta's portrait of Firoz is altogether different: on his very first day he ransomed the prisoners taken during the confusion, on the third he marched out and defeated the treacherous mercenaries who had robbed the camp, and thereafter he turned to building — canals, cities, markets, colleges — and to easing the weight of rule on his people. In one volume the reader passes from a tyrant who 'nourished a serpent in his bosom' to a builder sultan who gladdened the court with presents and governed to be remembered.

Figures: Sultan Mahummud Tuglick (Muhammad bin Tughlaq), Firoze Shaw (Firoz Shah Tughlaq), Amir Norose, Altu Chan

The story as a comic

1
A riverside barge in the last days of a reign: a sick man drooping over a plate of fish while his army waits ashore.
Courtier: Sire, the fever — let the army halt.
Sultan Mahummud: We press on for Tatta.
Twenty-seven years of 'labouring to be feared and abhorred by all men' — ended by a fever after eating fish to excess.
2
A banquet hall strewn with assassinated nobles; a messenger reporting to a satisfied Sultan on his throne.
Messenger: Eighty of them, Sire, with their attendants.
Sultan: Send the doer a robe and a fine horse. Well rewarded.
'Such were the morals of those wretched days!' — his general rewards an eighty-man massacre.
3
The tyrant raising a gardener, a singer, a weaver and a slave to the rank of omrah before the scowling nobility.
The poet (caption): He who exalts a beggar's head nourishes a serpent in his bosom.
He promoted men of low birth to command provinces — one of many resolves born of the nobles' refusal to obey cruelly.
4
The deathbed: the Sultan commending his nephew Firoze Shaw to the omrahs.
Sultan Mahummud: Let Firoze succeed me.
On his death-bed the tyrant names his nephew — and the army falls into confusion.
5
Night: Mogul mercenaries loading a camel with the dead Sultan's treasure while the camp burns behind them.
With the king dead, his mercenary allies loot the imperial treasure and make for home.
6
Firoz on a borrowed throne, pressed by kneeling omrahs; beyond him, ransomed prisoners returning through the gate.
The Omrahs: Mount the throne, Sultan — we have no other.
Firoze Shaw: Then it is mine. First, the prisoners; next, the thieves of Tatta.
His first days: free the captives, then crush the mercenaries who robbed the camp.
7
A serene Delhi skyline taking shape: a canal being dug, a new city wall, a college, a market; Firoz directing with a map.
The builder-sultan inscribes his name in stone and water — cities, canals, colleges — the empire's greatest monument-maker.

Why it stands out

The same family produced, in quick succession, the most detested king the book describes and one of the most beloved. Ferishta's summing-up of Mahummud is among the harshest verdicts in the entire volume — 'detested by God, and feared and abhorred by all men' — and the contrast makes a vivid two-generation portrait of how kingship could swing between extremes.
