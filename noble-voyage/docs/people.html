The people — a cast of the book

Monarchs, slave-kings, queens and conquerors crossing Firishta's pages

The monarchs, slave-kings, queens and conquerors who cross Firishta's pages.

Charles Frederick Noble

fl. 1747–48

Author; officer in the East India Company's service

In the book: The preface promises the 'genuine production of a real traveller, an officer in the service of the East India Company… an accurate observer, who describes chiefly what he has seen.'

Editor's note: Beyond this one journey little is recorded of Noble himself — the book is almost all we know. 'Captain Charles Frederick Noble' appears on the title page; his promised value lies in being, as he stresses, an eyewitness rather than an armchair compiler.

The King of Madura

dethroned c. 1747

Dethroned monarch of the island of Madura

In the book: Fled his own island when the Dutch deposed him, escaped their pursuers, and threw himself on the protection of the English ship Onslow — only to be 'tragically delivered' back to the Dutch amid a scene Noble calls one of the most melancholy ever acted in the East.

Editor's note: Madura, off the north coast of Java, had long been a vassal of the Dutch East India Company (VOC). Noble's account — of a king pleading for help and the crew weeping as he was led away — is a striking contemporary indictment of both Dutch 'insolence and barbarity' and 'English pusillanimity and misconduct.'

Captain Congreve

1747

Commander of the English ship 'Onslow'

In the book: The English captain who first sheltered the fleeing King of Madura, was imprisoned by the Sultan of Benjar, and was ultimately powerless when the Dutch demanded the king's surrender.

Editor's note: The 'Onslow' was an East Indiaman (an English Company trading ship). Noble's account shows how thin English protection could be in the waters the Dutch Company dominated — even a captain could end up in a native sultan's prison.

The Sultan of Benjar

1747

Ruler on Borneo who imprisoned Capt. Congreve

In the book: The sovereign to whom the trading port of Benjar belonged, who clapped the English captain in prison — a glimpse of the perils of Company commerce far from any European fleet.

Editor's note: Benjar is Banjarmasin, on the south coast of Borneo — a pepper port contested between Dutch and English traders in the 18th century.

The Female Volunteer

1747

A woman who slipped aboard ship

In the book: 'A Female volunteer gets aboard, but discovered' — one of the book's earliest and most human curiosities, a woman managing to reach the outward-bound vessel during the press at Gravesend.

Editor's note: The Thames 'press' of men for outward East-Indiamen was unruly, and the occasional woman did manage to smuggle herself aboard. Noble's passing mention is a small gem of social history.

The Emperor of China

reigned 1735–1796

Sovereign of the Empire of China

In the book: Noble describes the emperor's title, his palace and throne at Pekin, his person and dress, and the ceremonial manner of receiving ambassadors.

Editor's note: In 1747–48 the Dragon Throne was held by the Qianlong Emperor — one of the longest-reigning and most powerful monarchs in world history, who received no embassy from the English until 1793. Noble's account reflects what a merchant saw of that grand and distant court.

The Governor of Batavia

1747

Chief magistrate of the Dutch capital at Batavia

In the book: Noble dwells on 'the grandeur of its governor,' the state and ceremony with which the head of the Dutch settlement at Batavia kept court.

Editor's note: The Governor-General of the Dutch East Indies ruled from Batavia (modern Jakarta) what was arguably the richest corporation-state on earth — a private company with fleets, armies and provinces.
