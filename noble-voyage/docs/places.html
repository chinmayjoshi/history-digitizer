The places of the book

Cities, frontiers, rivers and holy places on Firishta's stage

The places where the story unfolds, with their modern settings.

St. Helena

Saint Helena, South Atlantic (British territory)

First landfall; an impregnable island rock

In the book: The lonely island where the voyage first touched — famed for being 'impregnable,' thick with rats and abounding with fish, home to a tiny, none-too-learned society of whites and slaves.

Editor's note: A century later St. Helena would hold one of history's most famous prisoners, Napoleon. In Noble's day it was a vital refreshment stop for outward-bound Indiamen and a bleak island prison.

The Straits of Sunda

Selat Sunda, between Sumatra and Java

Sea-gate from the Indian Ocean to the Java Sea

In the book: The passage through the Straits of Sunda, fourteen days under way, where the pinnace was sent ashore and returned with a stray bottle of 'letters of intelligence.'

Editor's note: Today a busy shipping lane, Sunda was then the Dutch Company's guarded doorway to the archipelago — the straits the vulnerable English Indiamen had to run.

Java

Java, Indonesia

The great island of the Dutch Indies

In the book: Noble's account of the island and its inhabitants and climate — the prows of the native craft and the government under the Dutch.

Editor's note: Java, whose interior rice-wealth and spice coasts the VOC ruled through native princes, was the centre of the most valuable overseas empire the world had then seen.

Batavia

Jakarta, Indonesia

Capital of the Dutch East Indies; 'the great emporium of the Dutch and of India'

In the book: The city of canals and warehouses, its grandeur and its governor, the plague of the 'Mutqueto' fly, and the infamous Dutch thief-catchers who eagerly pounced on escaped Company soldiers.

Editor's note: Batavia had a justly deadly reputation for climate and sickness (dysentery and 'the fever'), yet it was the richest trading city between the Cape and Canton — the pivot of the VOC economy.

Madura

Madura Island, Indonesia

Island off Java; scene of the book's great tragedy

In the book: The little kingdom whose king the Dutch dethroned — the episode Noble calls 'one of the most melancholy scenes that, perhaps, has ever been acted in the east.'

Editor's note: Madura's princes long chafed under VOC vassalage. Noble's account of the deposed king's plea and tragic handover is one of the modern period's most affecting descriptions of a native ruler undone by the East India Companies.

Benjar

Banjarmasin, Borneo, Indonesia

Pepper port on Borneo

In the book: The trading port whose sultan imprisoned Captain Congreve during the king of Madura's flight.

Editor's note: Banjarmasin, on Borneo's south coast, was a contested pepper port where the English and Dutch Companies schemed against one another through native rulers.

Canton

Guangzhou, China

The great port of the China trade

In the book: The teeming port described at length: fishes sold alive, dogs, cats and rats sold in the markets, blind beggars, the rude rabble, the author's attempt to glimpse the Chinese ladies, and the English factory where he was 'shampoed.'

Editor's note: In the 1740s Canton was the only Chinese port open to European trade — the single funnel of the entire China tea and silk trade out of which the greatest fortunes of the British Empire were built.

Pekin

Beijing, China

Capital of the Empire of China

In the book: Noble relates the emperor's title, his palace and throne, his person and dress, and the ceremony of receiving ambassadors.

Editor's note: The Forbidden City at Beijing was the political heart of a quarter of humanity — its courts and thrones the most elaborate centre of power in the 18th-century world.

Read: scan 158 · featured story

Tartary

Inner Mongolia / Central Asian steppe (Manchuria & Mongolia)

The northern steppe beyond the Great Wall

In the book: Noble's 'short Account of Tartary' — the vast northern territories of the Chinese empire and its steppe peoples.

Editor's note: The Manchu conquerors of China — the Qing dynasty — came from 'Tartary'; the description captures how far the steppe frontier shaped the Celestial Empire.

The island of St. Paul

Île Saint-Paul, southern Indian Ocean

A lonely middle-ocean island sighted at sea

In the book: 'A view of the island of St. Paul at sea' — a bare volcanic speck in the great emptiness between the Cape and the Indies.

Editor's note: Still as desolate today as in 1747, St. Paul is a reminder of the vast expanse an Indiaman crossed with scarcely an island for a thousand leagues.
